1. How to distinguish genuine antique porcelain from fake

Antique porcelain items include artistic and historical value items, the age of which exceeds 100 years. The most ancient porcelain products with historical value are called antiques by professionals. Their age exceeds approximately 300 years or more.

The approximate determination of the time and place of manufacture of antique porcelain requires so much specialized knowledge and experience that even among experts in this field, there are many disagreements about their origin, approximate dating, and identification.

Features of antique porcelain products

Antique porcelain products have their own small market of collectors. Various items are regularly auctioned in New York and in Europe. Handmade, these ancient pottery items can most often be seen in museums, where they are protected by glass showcases from light, exposure to chemical elements, and visitors' ability to touch them.

The most famous is "tricolor irrigation ceramics" or "with multicolored/polychrome glaze in blurring spots ."Until the middle of the last century, "tricolor irrigation ceramics" remained known exclusively in literary descriptions and stylizations made already in the XV-XVII centuries. It is difficult to imagine that collectors initially ignored these ancient products. Their first authentic samples were found in 1949 in one of the burials in the vicinity of Luoyang (Henan Province).

Today it is established that this variety began to be produced in the last quarter of the VII century, and the heyday of its production fell on the first half of the VIII century. Ceramic products of this time directly reflected in their forms the contact of Chinese culture with the cultures of other countries. So, among the vessels of this era, there is form resembling the outlines of the nature of Greek amphora's neck handles. During the Tang period, yellow, brown glazes, and scarce blue (cobalt) appear along with green. Characteristic signs of antiquity are the thin craquelure on the surface of the ice, which is covered with a white coating. The bottom of the vessels is flat and unglazed.

The production of "tricolor irrigation ceramics" finally stopped in the Northern Song era. Perhaps this was due to the destruction of the centers to produce products of the San-Tsai style as a result of the wars. Still, it is also likely that the colorfulness of such ceramics came into conflict with the new aesthetic criteria established in Chinese art of the X--XIII centuries. Recently, collectors have been showing increasing interest in ceramic products of the Song dynasty (960-1279). The forms of such products are balanced and straightforward. Shot glazes have a muted color and the most delicate gray, blue, and green shades. Often the legs or bases of objects are not covered with frost. A vital element of the decoration of things of this period is the cap - small cracks covering monochrome products. This technique, which appeared, probably, as a defect during firing, was later used deliberately for decorative purposes. Chinese masters have learned to masterfully control the process of craquelure formation, which is formed due to the difference in the reduction coefficient of the shard and glaze when the mass of base breaks the ice when cooling. Ceramists even achieved delayed crackling, in which cracks of different thicknesses form on the product for months. To further accentuate the craquelure pattern, black and brown (the colors of "fried rice") paints were rubbed into them, which is especially characteristic of "gear" ceramics (created in special furnaces in Longquan). In addition, the "gear" used powder, not liquid glazes, so there are a lot of tiny bubbles in them.

The peculiarity of the technology of applying glaze is its abundance. Sometimes the shard was incredibly overlooked for the effect of "incompleteness ."This was a consequence of the Chan Buddhist views that the process itself is necessary, the path by which perfection is achieved, and the contemplator of ceramics should complete it during meditation.

During the Mongol rule under the Yuan Dynasty in the XIII-XIV centuries, the appearance of products with blue (cobalt) underglaze paintings was an innovation. Bright blue cobalt was brought from Persia at that time. The glaze is white and slightly matte. It is expressed in a confident stroke or line with the desired bend or thickening. Inimitable perfection is achieved by artists' skill in possession of a brush.

The Ming Dynasty is distinguished by the following periods: early Ming (1368-1435), Interregnum (1436-1464), middle Ming (1465-1521), late Ming (1522-1627), the Transition period (1628-1644) is separately distinguished.

At the beginning of the Ming reign, porcelain became widespread. It is used to make large aquariums, garden stools, headrests ("porcelain pillows").

The most interesting from the point of view of the development of porcelain art are the reigns of the emperors Xuande (1426-1435), Chenghua (1465-1487), Wanli (1573-1620), and the Transition period.

The conquest of China by the Manchus and the founding of the Qing dynasty in the XVII-XIX centuries strengthened feudal relations and expanded the state's territory. The growth of maritime trade and the interest in the porcelain of the emperors led to the rise of porcelain production.

During the Qing Dynasty, the concentration of porcelain production in Jingdezhen increased. By order of the Kangxi Emperor in 1674-1678, the imperial factory was destroyed during the uprising against the Manchus. The plant receives huge palace orders, private production is also growing at the expense of foreign markets. Especially significant for the development of porcelain art were the reigns of the Kangxi emperors (1661-1722), Yongzheng (1723-11735), and Qianlong (1735-1796).

The porcelain decor technique takes its name from the various shades of greenery characteristic of this style. During the reign of the Kangxi Emperor, the production of porcelain in the type of "green family," "black family," and "yellow family" developed. The "green family" (famille verte) is a term adopted in the XIX century by French researchers (A. Jacquemart, Julie Blanc, E. Grandidier), who singled out Chinese porcelain products of the Kangxi period, decorated in the technique of polychrome painting using overglaze paints, into a separate group.

These paints were of a specific range: transparent green (two shades), yellow, blue, iron-based red, purple, and black.

The shard is distinguished by its exceptional whiteness and plasticity. Since the end of the XVII century, figured stamps, cartouches, medallions surrounded by finely designed ornaments have prevailed. Many literary subjects penetrate into porcelain painting, and painting and engraving on literary themes and literary and theatrical splints served as models for it. The high technique of copying from famous paintings that fed the piece of porcelain painting also contributed to this process.

The painting is characterized by several features: a simple differentiation of individual plots develops into a solid background with sharply highlighted reserves or medallions for another theme, often made in a different colorful scale. For the products of the "green family," the background ornament is very diverse: a spiral brown-black microscopic pattern, along which red or gold peonies or other flowers are scattered; geometric decoration, for example, a mottled grid of connected octahedrons and squares or of intersecting lines forming a diamond-shaped pattern.

The geometric grid acquires many options. Framing the central theme, which usually consists of either flowers or a symbolic scene, is divided into many segments. The painting is characterized by incredible naturalism, up to the image of flaws, for example, holes made by a snail on lotus leaves, while small plant motifs are distinguished by a thoughtful composition. The exception is ferruginous red paints, used underglaze for contours that highlight structures and gilding.

The technical characteristic of the authenticity of the entire "green family" is the "halo effect," or the presence of overflows at the top of the glaze and surrounding enamels, which is especially pronounced around the blue enamel. This iridescence resembles oil stains on water and is visible when light falls on the ice in a certain way.

Imitations of Kangxi products begin in the middle of the XVIII century. A series of vases on the theme "memory of Kangxi" is being created at the imperial factory.

The appearance in the "green family" of a black background covered with green glaze, on which images of flowers, rocks, birds stand out effectively, led to forming a new group, conventionally called the "black family. There is less white natural background in reserve, which is replaced by applying white body enamel. ."In the "black family," the enamel acquires a strong chandelier coating. This distinguishes it from the classic Kangxi.

The "green family" subgroup, the distinguishing feature of the yellow background, is referred to as the "yellow family."

The period of Yongzheng's reign marked the beginning of the development of the "pink family" style - Famille rose (fr.) This is a term adopted in the XIX century for a later variety of polychrome painting with pale pink enamel's most delicate tonal transitions, which appeared in China around the 10th-20th century. French researchers (A. Jacquemart, Le Blanc, E. Grandidier) introduced a term to designate Chinese porcelain products decorated in the technique of overglaze polychrome painting using diluted (mixed with white enamel) opaque paints in combination with a specific pink based on colloidal gold. Compared to the transparent "green family," this new opaque palette could be fired at a lower temperature, making it possible to apply a broader range of colors. The palette has become softer and more gentle.

2. Evaluation of Chinese antique porcelain products

Algorithm of evaluation and diagnostics of antique porcelain products

It consists of the following:

  1. Product inspection.
  2. Determination of the composition (porcelain, faience, stone mass).
  3. Diagnostics of the manufacturing period.
  4. Study of a set of brands and stamps.
  5. The study of stylistic features.
  6. Studying the features of the technology.
  7. Calculation of the base cost.
  8. Determination of increasing coefficients.
  9. Product wear assessment.
  10. Market research.
  11. Determination of the market value of the product.

There are some peculiarities in determining the value of antiques. So, when evaluating ancient products, the assessment must begin with the appearance, and then the quality of manufacture is considered, the technologies used and the features of the decoration are analyzed.

A significant part of the early ceramic products were products of the Tang era in the "sansai" technique, which can be valuable in themselves. The monetary value of the products is quite controversial and depends mainly on the collector's desire to purchase the product and pay the required amount. The current market sets the value of ancient products from the Northern Dynasties period (550-577) and the Tang Dynasty period (618-907). Prices can fluctuate significantly due to several factors:

- the item's rarity relative to other known things of the same type

- the time it was on the market

- the materials used in its manufacture

- the quality of work

The market consists of private collectors and museums.

It is necessary to keep in mind one feature: Asian collectors are looking for items from later dynasties (Song, Ming, Qing), and for Western collectors in recent years, ancient products and objects from graves have also become interesting.

For an appraiser to determine the value of an antique or antique product, he needs a deep knowledge of the culture and social customs of the corresponding era.

The basis for the evaluation of antique Chinese porcelain is determining the time of creation and the place of manufacture. If there is a set of brands on the product, they are defined using unique reference books. When establishing antiquity, the stamp is the essential characteristic. In fakes, the symbols often do not coincide with the genuine ones because the forgers did not pay any attention to them, and sometimes they did not have the necessary information. If there are no stamps and inscriptions, the diagnosis is carried out by studying the stylistic features.

Each piece of porcelain should be carefully studied both from the stylistic and technical sides. It also happened that the item did not have signs or stamps at all, although it was copied accurately. In this case, the product should be subjected to a thorough study from the point of view of style. The style and shape should match the decor. There are often contradictions between the form of the vessel and its ornamentation. In the figured set, there is a discrepancy in the theme, the vestments of the figures, the landscape, and the stylistics of the scenes. The bottom of the vessels and handles were used differently in different periods.

Distinctive features will be analyzed by the example of flattened handles, which decorated vases from the middle of the XVIII century. The handles are made in simplified schematic bodies of dragons, forming a kind of curl in the lower part. Such whimsical handles are an important stylistic feature of the vessels of the second half of the Qianlong reign.

If you have a product with handles and stickers, ask yourself: do they correspond to this period?

Then you should check the manufacturing method of this product.

The methods of manufacturing modern mass production differ from the old technologies. Manufacturing everyday porcelain products is carried out by pouring liquid porcelain mass into absorbing plaster molds. This mass is left in the mold for several minutes until the slip layer hardens. The excess of the still liquid clay is then poured out, and the product is left to dry. The finally solidified element is removed from the mold and dries in the air. This method makes it easy to produce porcelain products with a thin body.

Antique porcelain products, like other ceramic ones, were created on a potter's wheel, and if the product had square or curly shapes, then with the help of wooden plates. The still wet pieces were put together with a small amount of clay of a creamy consistency. The most round and tall ancient Chinese porcelain vessels, as a rule, were assembled from two or three horizontal sections and joined together to give them the final shape.

The final shape is achieved by mounting on a potter's wheel and being cut using turning tools. The handles and neck of the vase were made separately, and only then were the handles attached to the vase. A meaningful diagnostic sign of the identification of antiquity is the search for traces of slickering (gluing or combining two or more sections of an unfired ceramic element using liquid clay -slicker). In original antique porcelain works, you can see traces of gluing the vessel. Such a connection can be seen as gluing and especially felt inside. This is usually found on the shoulders of tall vases and in the most comprehensive places of the belly of the flask.

The next step is to search for firing defects. Historically, the biggest challenge for potters is finding the correct firing temperature. If the temperature is too high, the porcelain melts and deforms. To stop the deformation, kaolin is added to the clay. In the XV- XVI centuries, the content of kaolin in export items and products for the people was low for economic reasons, so contorted forms can often be found.

On the products of the transition period (XVII century), the following defects were often formed after firing: cracks, pimples, blisters, blockage, red or yellowish tint. The study of such defects helps to diagnose a porcelain object.

Not all antique porcelain products have the same artistic value. Learn to distinguish the good from the beautiful and the beautiful from the exceptional. The only way to gain such experience is to study this style for a long time and study as many porcelain products decorated with enamels as possible.

It is necessary to understand the peculiarities of the technology of applying enamels to a porcelain mold. Enamel is an alloy of lead, tin, various oxides with borax, glass powder, and quartz.

A key component of vitreous enamel is a very loose form of glass called frit.

Enamel pigments in their natural state are a dry powder usually mixed with oil, glue, or ordinary water.

The paints are applied gently with sweeping brush strokes when using the painting. If enamel is applied to a porcelain object, it is necessary to see if it is in good condition and if there are any chips and traces of replacing individual enamel sections with varnish. Check how clean and clear the drawing is and whether the work is done cleanly. Pay attention to the contours of the picture. At the same time, it should be remembered that temporary outlines can be used on dishes to guide decorators later they will burn during firing.

In China, several glazes and enamels began to be used only in the first third of the XVIII century, such as brown chocolate with a chandelier shade. Later this enamel will frame the brands in products with compositions in the range of "pink family ."At the same time, faded enamels of rare shades spread: pistachio-green, olive, ivory, pinkish. Accordingly, if there are enamels in the painting - white, pink, purple, violet-blue, and the stamp indicates the reign of the Ming dynasty, then the thing is imitative since these enamels appeared only at the turn of the XVIII century.

At the same time, free cobalt painting begins to combine with monochrome glazes (a technological achievement as a result of double firing). At the same time, glazes of the color of burnt sugar are included in the decor in the form of belts emphasizing the vessel's shape. There are often products in which individual parts are highlighted with a similar glaze. In European countries, it was called differently: "Batavian brown", "cappuccino", "dry leaf". Products with such decoration are in great fashion in Holland, where the decorator promoted them from France Daniel Moreau (1661-1752). Therefore, the term "Moreau style" is also used in relation to this color.

You can select products of the "cloisonne" style (French cloison - partition) in a separate group. This style of ceramic or porcelain decoration resembles cloisonne enamel on metal, where the rim is used to outline the decoration areas. This style of decoration originated in the reign of Jungian. Clay flagella were laid out according to the drawing, and the depressions formed by them were filled with colored glazes - purple, yellow, blue, and turquoise.

When evaluating antique porcelain products with gilding, appraisers need to understand and distinguish the type of gilding. The most ancient gilding technique is "solid gilding" or "fire-gilding." Pure gold is mixed with liquid mercury, applied to the product, fired. When heated, mercury evaporates, and gold remains on the surface in the form of a matte coating, which must then be polished for shine and a bright finish. According to the reports of a Jesuit missionary from Jingdezhen in 1712 and 1722, gilding on Chinese porcelain was made by grinding gold leaves into powder and further mixing this powder with ordinary colorless enamel. Therefore, overlaying on products made before the middle of the XVIII century does not have a bright shine.

Another method of gilding was introduced in the West in the 1850s. This is "liquid gold," where powdered gold is applied as a liquid suspension and applied to porcelain.

When the product is pulled out of the furnace, the gold is bright and does not require polishing. The disadvantage of this method is that bright gold tends to rub off very quickly.

The attribution algorithm of porcelain

  1. Determination of the country of production.
  2. Determination of rarity for collections.
  3. Classification and description of the product.
  4. Analysis of technological features.
  5. Style analysis and brand study.

6 Critical analysis and identification of inconsistencies with the declared creation period.

  1. Determining the time of creation and attribution of the item.
  2. Artistic merits.
  3. Evaluation and attribution of porcelain.
  4. Rarity.

Analysis in the evaluation of antique porcelain is a mental and fixed dissection of an object or collection into separate components used during the study of an object.

1. Determination of the country of production.

Lifestyle, local traditions and technologies of manufacture and painting, varieties and features of raw materials, traditional products, and details help determine the place of pottery manufacture.

2. Determination of rarity for collections.

It is related to the classification of the purpose of the porcelain object. Almost all the variety of ancient Chinese porcelain products can be divided into three types: 1) ordinary products, as a rule, anonymous, interpreting the aesthetics of porcelain approximately, at the level of an ordinary collective image (mass production having a "typical" form); 2) export porcelain; 3) "iconic" products defining the line of development of porcelain art, approaching the personal sense of figurative characteristics to works of art.

Most Chinese porcelain is "ordinary" porcelain and is unlikely to be of interest to a collector. At the first acquaintance with the subject, it is necessary to determine whether it belongs to ordinary products (for the people). This type includes Chinese commercial porcelain products for trade, export, and local markets.

According to several signs (motifs, painting, forms), it is not difficult to distinguish export porcelain.

For a porcelain product that may be of value to the collection, it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive analysis of stylistic features and artistic merits.

In the process of studying ancient Chinese porcelain, the search for literary and illustrative sources, their analysis, classification, and fixation are significant. Learning sources develop an interest in stylistic analysis, activate attention, a sense of orientation when participating in auctions, and teach quick viewing and selecting what meets a high standard.

Evaluation of the "formal" aesthetic characteristics: harmony, color consistency, quality of execution - allows you to determine the rarity and originality of the object.

Analysis of technological features.

The visual diagnostic method of antique porcelain allows you to identify the features of the material: porcelain dough, glazes, paints, uniformity or heterogeneity of the product, and the size and uniformity of the bubbles on the ice.

When assessing the quality of an antique porcelain product, it is necessary to check whether there are molding defects. These include the thickness of the wall, tool marks, and foreign substances in the porcelain mass.

The signs of the antiquity of ancient Chinese porcelain are indicated by the technological features of manufacturing. These include:

1\) color and shade of porcelain dough,

2\) features of the glaze,

3\) features of the base (bottom),

4\) decoration technique (painted and monochrome porcelain),

5\) features of enameling,

6\) features of painting,

7\) signs of handwork (gluing lines, traces of a potter's wheel on the base).

When analyzing and diagnosing antique pottery, it is necessary to remember that porcelain is a ceramic impervious to water and gas. It is possible to distinguish porcelain products from products made of other ceramics by a clear, prolonged ringing or a high, clear sound when lightly struck with a wooden stick. Depending on the shape and thickness of the product, the tone may be different. Porcelain products in a thin layer are translucent; they are complex and vitreous.

Porcelain and "stone" ceramics differ fundamentally in the composition of the ceramic dough and the features of the technological process. A mandatory component of the porcelain mass is a "porcelain" stone (a rock of volcanic origin, a compound of feldspar with white mica). The production of "stone" ceramics involved the addition of quartz-containing substances, as which sand was most often used. Making "stone" ceramics did not require such thorough cleaning of components as porcelain production. Therefore, ceramic shards usually have a greenish, pale cream, or grayish-white color, noticeably different from the snow-white bit of high-quality porcelain (low-quality porcelain is sometimes characterized by minor color impurities).

The fundamental difference between "stone" ceramics and porcelain is their firing temperature. The firing temperature of "stone" ceramic varieties, depending on the time and geography of their manufacture, ranges from 1050 to 1250 °. The firing temperature of porcelain should be at least 1350 °, only then there is a qualitative transformation of the initial physical structure of the ceramic mass and, thanks to the silicon contained in kaolin clay, it becomes vitreous, translucent, and completely waterproof. When attributing, it is vital to determine the fineness and transparency of the shard.

Visual diagnostics allow you to identify the material: porcelain, earthenware, "stone" ceramics - according to the following signs:

\* color and shade of the dough,

\* texture, uniformity,

\* hardness,

\* transparency,

\* sound when tapping,

\* purity of the material.

A thorough examination of the glazes is mandatory for diagnosis.

4. Styles in Chinese antique porcelain products

The nature and forms of porcelain products reflect the life and development of human society, ideology, culture, and energy of a specific era. This is their historical significance. Over time, ceramic products acquire characteristic differences. The emergence and development of a style are influenced by many factors.

1. Class differences.

With the development of class relations, porcelain products receive more and more new meanings. They reflect not only the peculiarities of culture and differences in social status. It is known that there was a time when legislative measures established the use of specific porcelain products by estates.

Thus, the image of the five-fingered dragon in the Middle Qing era (XVIII century) retained the meaning of the symbol of imperial power, adopted at the end of the reign of Yongle (1403 -1424), and served as an indication that products with such a motif were intended for the Court. They were produced up to the beginning of the XX century, a kind of normative marking of the owner's status. Vessels decorated with an engraved image of a dragon under a yellow glaze of a characteristic warm shade, called "imperial yellow," could only be used by the emperor, empress, or Dowager Empress. All members of the imperial house used dragons of a specific color as a symbol of social status and rank.

In the decor of the official Chinese costume during the Qing period, all elements were strictly regulated, particularly the number of claws. For example, the emperor could "grant" the fifth claw to an official for merit. In this regard, in the painting of porcelain on imperial products, five-headed dragons were mandatory, and on private products, you can find mainly four-headed ones.

In China, belonging to a specific class was emphasized by the color of dragons in the images on porcelain. The idea of dragons is perhaps the most popular subject in painting, relief modeling, or engraving.

During the Qing Dynasty, the ruler ordered specially for the palace to make porcelain bowls decorated with dragons in different color combinations depending on the rank of the user:

\* Yellow dragon inside and out - for the Emperor, Empress, and Empress Dowager.

\* Dragons on a yellow background, white background inside - for officials of the first rank.

\* Green dragons on a yellow background, white background inside - for the wife of the second and third rank.

\* Yellow dragons on a blue background, white background inside - for the wife of the fourth rank.

\* Eggplant-colored dragon and green dragons on yellow background-for members of the royal family and the wife of the fifth rank.

\* Red dragons with polychrome painting. For a spouse of the sixth rank.

\* Polychrome painting - for the wife of the seventh rank.

2. The influence of personality.

The epoch that created the style is characterized not only by the scope of artistic activity but also by the success and talent of individual artists. Individual outstanding personalities played a significant role.

Such a historical figure was Tang Yin (1682-1756), the caretaker of the imperial porcelain factory in Jingdezhen. He remained in this position for more than two decades.

Under his leadership, the porcelain in Jingdezhen has reached an unprecedented level. Tang Yin created the "luxurious style--- - the stylistic originality of the imperial porcelain of the Qianlong era. In the design of objects of that time, artists often practice the carpet (solid) decoration principle, combining various techniques. The "luxurious style" was distinguished by the maximum (in comparison with previous periods) complexity and detail of forms, as well as the saturation of decorative motifs.

6. Attribution of products painted with underglaze cobalt

A separate category of ancient Chinese porcelain products includes underglaze painting with cobalt, copper, or iron paint (metal oxides of iron, cobalt, and copper). Paints are applied to the ceramic product before it is coated with glaze.

Recent studies show that the features of the paint color and the application of cobalt painting can help determine cobalt sources and the product's production time. Guttetborg, on his website "Antique Chinese porcelain," gives a brief classification of cobalt :

1. The Tang Dynasty.

It is cobalt with a high copper and iron content and a low manganese content. Rich and dark blue color. It is assumed that there were several deposits of it: Persia (Iran, Afghanistan), India, Egypt, Western Asia Minor, and sediments along the shores of the Mediterranean Sea.

2. The Song Dynasty.

Cobalt with high manganese content and low iron content. After firing, it acquires a grayish-blue color and black inclusions. Its sources were located mainly in the province in the east of China.

3. The Yuan Dynasty.

It is very different between the early and late periods. Imported cobalt from the Middle East is used for painting large products. It has high lead content and low manganese content. The proper firing gives a bright blue color, sometimes with apparent iron spots (rust). Sometimes there is a blue color with a purple tinge.

4. The Ming Dynasty.

Due to the Civil war, the import of cobalt stopped, and the quality of the underglaze blue painting became very low. Only local cobalt was used in the production of porcelain. When the masters switched to domestic raw materials because cobalt was unrefined and contained many iron and manganese oxides impurities, the drawing had a grayish coating or numerous black dots. Folk products with illustrations have gray and dark shades. In a later period, the cobalt murals became brighter. But the blue painting sometimes has black-brown rust spots, called "heaping and piling ."This effect is that the surface of the glaze shows dark, purple-black spots due to the high concentration of pigment or impurities in cobalt. The product is characteristic of porcelain painting in the Yongle period (1403-1423).

The best are considered to be multi-layered cobalt paintings in the reign of Chenghua (1466-85) and Jiajing (1522-66) when imported "Muslim" blue paint was used, which was imported from Iraq.

At the beginning of the reign of Emperor Wanli, state-owned enterprises switched to blue cobalt with a purple tint, and private furnaces used mainly local bluish-gray or blackish-gray cobalt.

At the end of Wanli's reign and during the transition period (until the end of the Ming), state-owned factories used blue with shades of gray, light, and clear cobalt. Folk furnaces used many sources of cobalt. Bright purple can rarely occur.

Due to the lack of funds to purchase cobalt paint abroad, the masters switched to domestic color, which immediately affected the quality of the painting. Some changes in the shade of cobalt, which began to have a grayish coating or dark inclusions, gave rise to a fashion for products with a similar flavor. Such shades of paint were called "old cobalt" or "pre-transition cobalt" (1620 - 1670). The "old cobalt" peculiarity is a vague pattern, grayish tone.

5. The Qing Dynasty.

At the end of the XVII century, along with the additional processing of cobalt, magnets began to remove iron. The process of purification of local cobalt paints became more perfect. It led to the invention of bright blue cobalt "Tsinghua" in the Kangxi period of the Qing Dynasty.

Cobalt painting of the Yongzheng period was used for the contours of products in the style of "doucai ."Cobalt is used in faded tones and bluish-gray "pervansh" (pervansh is one of the shades of blue, grayish---blue, pale blue with a lilac tinge).

Products painted with cobalt from the Qianlong period were created to imitate the Ming period. Still, they have a more precisely laid elegant line, unlike the latter. The cobalt painting of the Qianlong period is distinguished by an exceptionally high quality of execution and bright saturated color. This is achieved primarily by peculiar techniques when the image is outlined with the thinnest cobalt line. A gentle cobalt blur is applied to the specified boundaries. A scattering of the smallest, darker cobalt dots is placed on the light background. The points are given by compact, logically placed groups. Cobalt placer is usually combined with precise shapes. It emphasizes the articulation of the vessel, accentuating the neck, shoulder, trunk, leg, breaking into peculiar concentric belts.

As a rule, cobalt products are of good quality and necessarily have a brand that can be trusted since they were created at imperial factories.

In the porcelain painting of the XIX century, dark cobalt dots that mimic the effect of "textural" paint in underglaze cobalt painting, which received the definition of "heaping and piling" in English literature (to draw and enhance), are distributed evenly over the entire surface of the paint layer, which gives the impression of some dryness and unnaturalness and can be taken into account when dating.

Vitaly (five colors of enamel). However, despite the name of the painting, the number of flowers in wucai decorations is not strictly limited to five. 7. Export porcelain

Chinese porcelain for export is defined as porcelain for use outside of China. Export porcelain of the XVIII and XIX centuries is often found on the market. And therefore, there are practically no fakes.

Today, the trade-in Asian export art objects leads them back to their homeland. Ceramics, furniture, and decorative and applied art created in the period from the beginning of the XV to the middle of the XIX century in the Far East specifically for export to the West are now increasingly attracting the attention of collectors from the countries of origin.

Recently, porcelain export in the antique markets has been classified as "Chinese art of the East India Company."

What does the term "East India Company" mean? The East India Company is the name of several trading societies in European countries of the colonial era. Since the end of the XVI century, maritime trade has been expanding. Chinese porcelain was in great demand at auctions in Europe, despite the high prices it was sold. Nevertheless, the popular objects in Europe were made specifically for export. They were of much lower quality than porcelain for the imperial court.

Vast quantities of porcelain, necessary to meet the enormous demand, have been transported from southern China to Batavia by Chinese junks since it became fashionable in Europe. The transportation of Chinese porcelain was carried out mainly by Chinese merchants, whose junk ships plied to the Philippines, the Indonesian archipelago, Vietnam, Thailand, and Malaysia. Dutch or other Europeans could privately join the Chinese crew from time to time. Still, indeed, none of the ships of the East India Company sailed to China at the beginning of the XVIII century to buy porcelain in Canton. All the risk in this process fell on the Chinese side, the Dutch did not pay transportation costs, and competition kept prices low. In 1729, the company's ships began to ply directly from the Netherlands to Canton and back.

Batavia (today's Jakarta) was an important market and a center for distributing porcelain to Holland and other ship-supplied markets, such as the Islamic markets of India, Indonesia, and Iran. The Dutch East India Company had its administrative and trading divisions in this city, which attracted trade from all over Asia. Batavia served partly as a fulcrum for further inter-Asian work and as a departure point for ships returning to the Netherlands.

Since 1757, Canton (now Guangzhou -the economic center of Southern China) has remained the only port open to foreign trade. In the XVIII century, ships of most European states came here. Among the exported goods, a significant place is occupied by porcelain, with which the port shops were crowded. But at the same time, foreign buyers were not satisfied with finished products that had a purely Chinese character but gradually began to place orders for the execution of specific forms and painting products that were unconventional for Chinese masters.

Jingdezhen factories at that time remained the leading supplier of the court of the Chinese emperors. But gradually, they also became the primary source of export porcelain. Merchants made their substantial orders through Canton. Burnt porcelain blanks were sent to the Canton workshops from Jingdezhen, which were then painted with overglaze enamel paints. This was caused by the need to contact the customer and the mass buyer directly. At the same time led to widespread penetration into Chinese workshops of engravings and drawings, which were very carefully copied on porcelain by Chinese painters.

There was a practice to place orders for a range of goods with a particular pattern through a Chinese captain, who passed the order to the porcelain workshops in Jingdezhen and waited for the items to be delivered to Batavia. Jingdezhen, which had been the center of porcelain production for centuries, consisted of hundreds, if not thousands, of small private workshops where porcelain was produced and painted. Workshops that produced porcelain for export had to work so that the goods looked attractive to foreign customers. They often made unique things to order and did not produce goods for the local market.

Murals were used in workshops where porcelain was made or in special workshops where it was also painted subject to specialization. It seems that there was a unique specialization when one workshop made only teapots for export, and the other - cups and saucers. The products of these workshops were fired in large furnaces, which were bought from the owners.

Manufacturers and owners did not distribute the final product themselves. Still, they used licensed brokers who made deals with Chinese merchants who distributed orders. It can be assumed that being small and specialized, these workshops producing export goods were not the first followers of fashionable trends in porcelain painting, which were larger and more important factories. It is very possible that they continued to work as they had been working for years, only gradually getting used to new trends and new themes in paintings only when their products were already outdated and caused complaints from merchants and brokers.

The forms and paintings of porcelain were adapted to foreign samples. The first European subjects were of a religious nature. It was often a figure or an image of Christ, but there was also a secular theme. The custom of ordering porcelain depicting the coats of arms of noble families in China has spread widely. The copying of European engravings and drawings and the depiction of Europeans from nature were not mechanical but processed.

When North America began sending ships for independent trade with China in the early 1800s, they established export products with a limited number of standard templates. Popular models were painted with underglaze cobalt called "Nanjing" with pagodas and river landscapes. Enamels with a pattern of flowers, birds, butterflies, and tangerines in the panels were called "rose medallion" and "mandarin medallion."

The products of private workshops, and at an earlier time distinguished by the greater coarseness of both material and execution, have become even more careless: coarse mass, grayish glaze. Roughness, grains of sand are constantly found on the base, and even the bottom of the cobalt export items was not always covered with frost. The division of labor has also spread widely to the execution of the painting. The more vital masters performed the basic sketch, the weaker specialized in specific details. There is also no doubt that the painting process was divided between artisans by colors -- one worked red, the other blue, etc. D.

Western export porcelain is easy to recognize due to its specific shapes and painting. Images of tangerines, flowers, and butterflies are characteristic.

Huge trays and tall water jugs were made for Turkey, Persia, and Islamic markets. For the countries of the Middle East, products with Chinese painting were produced, but of a specific shape (incense burners, incense burners) with Arabic or Persian inscriptions. For Central Asia - mainly bowls and plates with a kind of painting: large peonies, chrysanthemums, fruits that are not similar to those that were familiar to Chinese porcelain. Porcelain for Siam has an image of Buddhist deities. The porcelain made for the Japanese market was intended mainly for the Japanese tea ceremony: typical small objects deliberately made to look distorted.

Varieties of export porcelain

Among the many types of Chinese ceramics that have been exported, egg-shaped jugs are distinguished, which are sometimes called "martaban ."These large products have bold paintings in bright enamels, primarily olive green, golden brown, brown, or almost black. The name is associated with the transit port of Martypan in Burma, where there was a regular stop on the trade route of ships carrying this pottery.

Canton porcelain ("Canton" template)- the European name of the export porcelain made in Jingdezhen but painted in Canton in the first and second quarter of the XIX century. The design consists of a large central panel with a characteristic painting. There are "rose medallion" (characterized by four alternating panels around the main circle -medallion, the ornament contains peonies and groups of birds, butterflies, people in the house) and "mandarin medallion" - a term introduced for export porcelain, on which figures of mandarins (Chinese officials) were depicted in panels and surrounded by an abundance of flowers and ornaments covering the entire product. The term "Cantonese enamel" is also distinguished - only butterflies and flowers are present in the painting.

Chinese arita (or imari). The first Chinese Imari was produced in 1722-1725 and remained popular until 1760. By the 80s of the XVII century, when the situation in China stabilized and contacts with Europe resumed, Chinese craftsmen, first in Guangdong Province and then in Jingdezhen, began to reproduce products with characteristic blue-red painting, complementing it also with gold, pink, yellow and green enamels. Compared to Japanese, Chinese products have a more precise, symmetrical composition.

The state is an export ware of the Wanli period (1573-1620). It was produced at the end of the Ming Dynasty. The name comes from Shantou, a coastal city in the Southeastern province of Guangdong. The painting is characterized by iron-red, turquoise, and black colors.

Imperial porcelain - products of the highest quality, made by hand in state-owned (imperial) furnaces or in workshops. These products must correspond to the meaning of the words "magnificent" and "unique ."In these concepts, it is necessary to highlight the quality, the originality of the idea, the level of execution, the complexity of modeling, news in technology. At the same time, all details must be carried out exclusively cleanly, without technological defects. Special attention was paid to the elements, which should be of a very high level. Everything should have pleased the eye.

There were several requirements, the fulfillment of which makes it possible to optimally approach the evaluation of the imperial porcelain product:

\* quality and originality of the idea,

\* harmony of the composition,

\* clarity of geometric shapes,

\* correct pattern and symmetry of ornaments,

\* original silhouette,

\* beautiful texture,

\* color combinations of enamels and glazes

Vast deposits of first-class raw materials in the vicinity of Jingdezhen have contributed to the uninterrupted existence and development of the production of the best Chinese porcelain for many centuries. The central imperial orders, as well as orders for supplies to the foreign market, were received at this plant. In the Song era, the emperor ordered Jingdezhen to be designated as a special place for porcelain production, supplied only to the imperial palace. By the beginning of the XV century, the number of imperial furnaces has already reached fifty-eight.

The emperors of the Ming dynasty made high demands on the quality of products. To improve the molding properties of raw materials, the porcelain mass used to manufacture the famous Chinese porcelain with fragile walls was kept in a closed state in the ground for a hundred years.

Stamps on imperial porcelain

The thing made in the imperial furnaces has a high artistic and technological level. Her shard is free of impurities, clean and white, the glaze is shiny, the colors are applied (depending on the time of creation) neatly or artistically virtuosically. In this case, the brand can (just can!) testify to the product's authenticity.

It should be borne in mind that the imperial stamps were painted on porcelain with the help of a small number of highly specialized artists who spent their entire lives only writing signs on porcelain. These signs on various objects are well-known and recognized.

Imperial products with cobalt underglaze painting and board stamps are of high quality: their shapes are precise, emphasized by beautiful cobalt painting, the shard is slightly tinted, there is a scattering of black dots on the cobalt wash.

In 1680, about thirty workshops were organized within the walls of the Forbidden City in Beijing with the official task of producing works of art for the pleasure of the emperor and his court. In these workshops, experiments began with enamel, which decorated glass bottles for snuff. The main difficulty of manufacturing enameled glass was that the melting point of the enamels was almost the same as that of glass.

In 1777, these workshops began experimenting with the production of painted falangcai porcelain "foreign colors ."The superficial porcelain vessels were ordered from the imperial factory in Jingdezhen. The porcelain surface on which the enamels were applied was without the "biscuit" glaze. After the product was decorated with enamels, it was fired in a muffle furnace in the workshop of the palace. Apparently, during the experimental stage, the potters were still unable to overcome the technical problem associated with the different expansion/compression rates of enamels and porcelain surfaces, so Kangxi enamels are characterized by fragile cracks. Enameling was carried out on copper, porcelain, and glass in specialized workshops near the imperial living quarters in the Forbidden City and under the direct close attention of the emperor. The creation of workshops was initiated by European craftsmen who worked at the court of the Kangxi Emperor. He encouraged the production of new enamel colors to be applied in Beijing. This style of porcelain decoration was the property of only the emperor; simple potters did not have to know the technology.

During the Kangxi period, porcelain products had a colored base imitating the shape of cloisonne enamel on metal. My favorite color is red or yellow. The bottom has an overglaze enameled Kangxi sign.

During the reign of Yongzheng in 1728, additional colors of enamels began to be used in the imperial workshops in Beijing. The production of products depicting the compositions of "flowers-birds," accompanied by a poetic inscription and red seals, began. Since the workshops of the Beijing Palace have never been restrained by the need for large-scale production, their style of painting and coloring are marked by individuality, so all falangcai are delightful, but still there are differences in quality even among this select group of products. The brushstroke quality is equally close to ink paintings on paper or silk. The variety of enamel tones used, particularly for the plumage of birds, is remarkable. Unusual shades are mixed. The texture is visualized by applying thicker enamel, for example, birds' feet and claws.

Under Yongzheng, there was an imperial standard of elegance and sophistication in porcelain products. The emperor even appointed a specific team of artists to prepare drafts and sketches for painted enamel products.

Drawing styles, as a rule, show the influence of Western court artists who painted in a naturalistic manner. At the same time, the impression of volume, depth, and perspective was conveyed through shading. The realistic representation of birds and the shading of flowers show the direct influence of artists such as the Italian Jesuit missionary to China the painter at the Court Giuseppe Castiglione (1688-1766). His style was a unique blend of European painting with Chinese subjects and themes. It is assumed that he participated in the design of porcelain in the style of "falangasai".

The features of the painting of porcelain products "phalanges" of the Yongzheng period are a combination of a free and laconic composition with the careful spelling of details: petals, veins, flexible stems. Exquisitely abrupt documents appear, including a flower with a bud, bent blades of grass with insects, small flowers with a bee or two butterflies. At the same time, careful reliability of the modeling of objects is always achieved. The characteristic features of the falangasai tradition include a relatively rich color of pink enamel (later it is somewhat lightened) two shades of green enamel; the trunk of the trees is made of purple manganese enamel. The colors show subtle tonal transitions from crimson to light pink and white. During the reign of Qianlong in 1740, the image of people was added to the painting of falangasai products.

The works performed in the so-called "Hall of Prudent Virtue" are known. It is the primary residence of the Daoguang Emperor (1821-1850). The porcelain products made for this Hall are marked with a distinctive brand. Later, porcelain was also painted in the imperial workshops in Beijing.

Even the last self-proclaimed emperor of China, Yuan Shikai, who ruled from December 22, 1915, to March 22, 1916, gave orders to establish the production of imperial porcelain with his seal. These products' materials were used from imperial workshops, and the red seal "Jurentang" served as a label.

Some varieties and features of products from imperial furnaces

The bowl medallion. These bowls were used in the palace. The traditional point of view is that the blanks were made in Jingdezhen, decorated with an underglaze blue seal, and then sent to the imperial workshops in Beijing for painting with enamels. They were often decorated outside with four medallions protecting figures in landscapes.

Chrysanthemum. The name of the plates, shaped like a chrysanthemum flower. There is a real story that in 1733, the Yongzheng Emperor ordered the heads of the imperial oven in Jingdezhen to make chrysanthemum dishes of twelve colors. Later, the range of colors available for the enamel on porcelain was expanded due to the introduction of new pigments from Europe and experiments made in the workshops of the palace in Beijing and the porcelain workshops of Jingdezhen.

A premium vase (shan ping) with a slightly flared base on a thin neck, a ball body, and a stand. This style of vase first appeared during the reign of Yongzheng (1723-1735). The Chinese name of a vase with a spherical body and an extended base can be translated as "a vase that will make you happy." Such vases were first introduced in the Beijing period and became traditional imperial tableware. The primary colors of the glaze were blue and white and were treated as "pure and incorruptible ."The emperor granted them to officials he wanted to reward for "pure and incorruptible" behavior.

The imitative direction is a priority line of state-owned production. This is porcelain with the monochrome decor in underglaze painting with cobalt, combining images with cobalt and copper, and polychrome painting. The imitative trend, which was formed mainly in court art, is represented by various categories of replica products that address the achievements of ceramists of the past.

At the same time, copies (exact analogs), imitations (free repetitions close to the original), stylizations (interpretations that reproduce only the style, manner of painting, or the nature of the decor) are distinguished.

Continuous filling of the vessel surface.

The new decoration methods with polychrome enamels, which appeared during the reign of Qianlong, can be attributed to the constant filling of the surface with bright colored enamels with the preservation of reserves on the white background which a free composition was created. Sometimes the enamel background was supplemented with an engraving of sgraffito.

9. Varieties of old Chinese monochrome porcelain

Monochrome porcelain is ancient Chinese porcelain decorated with monochrome glazes from the Greek monochromes.

Initially, monochrome glazes were used to decorate altar vessels - a complex of sacrificial ritual equipment. The decree of the emperor of the Ming dynasty of 1369 prescribed the use of ritual utensils of the established pattern in temples of state significance, according to which monochrome porcelain was used for certain groups of altar vessels: blue for the altar of Heaven (Tiantan), yellow for the Earth (Ditan), red for the Sun (Chaozhitan) and white for the altar of the Moon (Siyuetan).

Under regulations established during the reign of the Ming Dynasty, which also operated in the Qing Empire, porcelain objects of specific categories were decorated with monochrome glazes of sacred colors, which led to a high level of their execution throughout the subsequent history of traditional China until the fall of the monarchy in 1911.

The potters of the imperial furnaces were obliged to experiment with new techniques and styles. They have created unique glazes of various colors and shades. Iridescent blue, purple, or brown splashes and spots characteristic of these monochromes were obtained due to the transmutation of colloidal solutions of copper, iron, or other metallic materials in the glaze surface under various firing modes. The masters masterfully controlled the firing process in the furnace, mastering both oxidative and reducing firing. Multiple shades of glazes and paints are formed. The problem is to catch the right temperature, and the right amount of air/smoke at certain moments.

Potters achieved especially great success in the manufacture of monochromes during the reigns of the Yongzheng emperors (1723-1735) and Qianlong emperors (1736-1795).

Red monochromes.

Especially it is necessary to highlight the red monochrome glaze with the minor cracks, distinguished by various shades, called "sacrificial red". This bright red glaze, even crimson due to the presence of copper during firing in a reducing atmosphere. The copper-red pigment is the most difficult to regulate during firing. The right conditions must be controlled to achieve rich crimson tones during the firing process. Copper-red glazes on porcelain products began to improve in China during the reign of Yongle (1403-1424). Initially, these items were used as imperial ritual vessels associated with the sacrifice to the Sun. At the end of the Ming Dynasty, copper-red glazes were less common.

Monochrome red was prevalent from 1723 to 1795. The glaze is smooth, opaque, and, as a rule, with an "orange peel" on the surface. It was standardized as a background for imperial products.

Shades and patterns of spots distinguish red glazes. The highest category includes products with glazes "big red robe" or "red cardinal" (bright red and "flawless," the second class comprises glazes "red bean," "red apple," "blush of beauty," and glazes with spots of different shades. The third category includes glazes with tonal transitions called "baby's face" or "peach blossom ."The lower types include glazes with the names "elm bark," "donkey liver," "light horses."

The classification features are specific. For example, if the red glaze is painted brightly and evenly, it is called a "big red robe," a slightly spotted glaze is called "drunken beauty ."If the color tends to reddish-pink (with dots), it is called "peach blossom."

A unique, rare category should include products with glaze "green lichen" or "variegated beans ."This glaze is based on copper oxides of a purplish-brown color with inclusions of greenish specks. The base of the ice is copper, and small green spots on a pinkish-red background are formed when the green copper oxide is not entirely restored to red. Green indicates pockets of accidental oxidation during firing, which is extremely rare. The firing of this kind was very difficult. These items were produced only in small quantities for the emperor's private orders. The products have never been higher than 20 cm. Their primary purpose is to decorate the scientist's desk. The primary color, which looks like a ripe cherry, changes during the firing process, giving many different shades. Sporadic is the appearance of green spots of non-circular shape (light shades of green, like green moss on the ground).

Somewhat later, by a conscious change in the firing methods, which was carried out at a high temperature, unusual shades began to be obtained, passing from red-purple to silver-blue on the same object. Such products are called flaming glaze in Europe - (flambée - flash). This specific kind of copper-red glaze (high-temperature reduction firing) with the addition of manganese, forming silver-purple streaks, was first obtained during the Kangxi period. This color is accepted as a result of firing when the glaze of copper oxide, which usually gives a green color under the influence of carbon specially introduced into the furnace, is restored to pure copper, shimmering with all shades of red, purple, and blue.

Blue monochromes

Blue monochromes come in various shades, mainly depending on the quality and quantity of cobalt used. They are classified by shades (light blue, dark blue, lavender, and gray-blue). They are fired in the same way as porcelain with blue painting, at high temperature.

Monochrome blue glazes come in several types, depending on how the ice is made and applied to the product. There are three main methods of application: dipping, painting, "splashed" cobalt.

Distinguish:

Dark blue "Mazarin ."The shade is a deep rich blue, found at the end of the XVIII and the beginning of the XVIII century on export porcelain, often with gilding. Perhaps the name comes from Cardinal Mazarin, Prime Minister of France (1601-1662). The same blue color was used as a background in the painting with reserves.

"Splashed" cobalt (French bleu poudré, English powder blue). The technique of decorating Chinese porcelain products is spraying (spraying) cobalt pigment on an unburned shard through a bamboo tube, followed by coating with glaze and firing and invented in the Kangxi period. The difference between the varieties of blue ice is the size of the cobalt particles. The "powder blue" variety has spots on the products, visible as powder, which create a mottled pattern.

"Blue gemstone" (Baoshan). It is found on products made in imperial furnaces. It is characterized by a velvety deep, uniform blue tone. The color is clean; the surface is greasy and thick, the texture of "orange peel" is noticeable.

Varieties of monochromes

Complex firing techniques were resumed in the 20-the 30s of the XVIII century, giving an unpredictable result. Bright green, pale lime green colors appear, products with blue with crimson spots or streaks of glaze appear, vessels with bluish and peacock green frosted glazes.

Distinguish:

Blanc de chine is white porcelain made during the Ming Dynasty.

Glaze "claire de lune" (fr. "light of the moon") is a glaze of a delicate bluish-greenish color with a low cobalt content (about 1%). It first appeared in the Kangxi period and was produced at the imperial furnaces in Jingdezhen for a limited number of forms. The Chinese call this shade of pale blue "the sky after the rain" or use the French expression "the moon is white ."Vases with this glaze were made as gifts for members of the imperial family.

Lavender is a blue-pink monochrome glaze. It was developed during the Qing Dynasty and was used only on imperial products.

"Black Mirror" (English: black mirror) is a black monochrome glaze that glitters like a mirror. It is rare and time-consuming. It requires high-temperature firing based on cobalt-ferrous ore obtained during the Kangxi period. Black products were often painted with gold.

Turkish blue or kingfisher colors - bright turquoise glaze. Different shades and tones of this glaze covered the inner parts of the vases and their bases.

Matte blue is a matte glaze that has a blue color. The ice was used on imperial wares.

"Peacock Green" is a glaze with a matte deep, bright green color with a hint of blue. It was used on imperial products.

Tea leaf powder - (English tea dust - tea dust). Olive-green two-layer spotted glaze. It is obtained by blowing the finest particles of green ice onto a yellowish-brown or dark green glaze. It is fired at a high temperature. The greasy surface of the glaze tends to have a yellow tint. It is evenly covered with tiny black and gray specks. The models are stately, elegant, and straightforward. The base usually has embedded four characters of the Yongzheng mark in two columns. There are shades under the names "yellow eel," "green snake," "green crab."

Robin's egg' glaze -- eggs of a bird) is a pock-marked opaque glaze of turquoise color with uneven crimson and purple spots. It was developed in 1723-35.

Partridge eggs are a pock-marked white glaze with black-brown spots. They were developed in 1723-1735.
