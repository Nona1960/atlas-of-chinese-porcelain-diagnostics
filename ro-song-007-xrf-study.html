A Southern Song Guan-Type Bamboo-Neck Vase: Portable X-Ray Fluorescence Analysis, Semi-Matte Celadon Glaze, Fine Crackle, and Comparative Archaeometry of the Imperial Kilns of Hangzhou

Professor Nona Dronova — Independent Researcher — Zenodo, 2026 — Original Research Article · Chinese Ceramic Archaeometry · Southern Song Official Kilns

← Back to the Atlas entry ro-song-007 Zenodo record · doi:10.5281/zenodo.23816980 XRF protocol (PDF)

Abstract

This article presents the results of a non-destructive archaeometric study of a Southern Song Guan-type celadon vase distinguished by a bamboo-jointed neck flaring towards the mouth, a rounded body, a grey-olive semi-matte glaze, fine surface crackle, and a contrasting dark ring zone at the base.

The object, designated Sample No. 8, was examined by macroscopic photography, optical documentation of the surface, the instrument’s targeting image, and portable X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (pXRF) carried out directly on the glazed body.

The pXRF analysis recorded silicon at 36.54 wt%, calcium at 8.953 wt%, aluminium at 6.52 wt%, potassium at 3.914 wt%, and iron at 0.673 wt%. Minor and trace constituents include manganese (523 ppm), titanium (680 ppm), rubidium (660 ppm), strontium (117 ppm), zirconium (76 ppm), and barium (356 ppm).

The theoretical oxide equivalents of the principal elements include approximately 78.17 wt% SiO₂, 12.53 wt% CaO, 12.32 wt% Al₂O₃, 4.72 wt% K₂O, and 0.96 wt% Fe₂O₃. These are independent stoichiometric conversions of the elemental readings and do not represent a normalised glaze composition.

The results confirm a substantial calcium-bearing silicate component and a relatively low iron concentration in the examined grey-olive glaze. These characteristics are discussed in the context of archaeological and experimental studies of Southern Song Guan ware from Laohudong and Jiaotanxia in Hangzhou.

The study also documents a fine, locally visible crackle network and a visually complex semi-matte surface. Together, the chemical, optical, and morphological data provide an original analytical description of a bamboo-neck celadon within the technological tradition of the Southern Song official kilns.

Keywords: Southern Song dynasty; Guan ware; official kilns; bamboo-neck vase; celadon; Laohudong; Jiaotanxia; portable X-ray fluorescence; pXRF; calcium-rich glaze; semi-matte glaze; crackle; optical microscopy; archaeometry; Chinese ceramics.

1. Introduction

Southern Song Guan ware occupies a central place in the history of Chinese imperial ceramics.

After the Song court moved from northern China to Lin’an (present-day Hangzhou) in 1127, the imperial administration developed ceramic production to supply the palace with vessels for ceremonial, ritual, and everyday use.

The official-kiln tradition known as Guan yao (官窑) became associated with monochrome celadon glazes, restrained vessel forms, thick glaze layers, a characteristic crackle network, and dark ceramic bodies.

Unlike ceramic traditions that emphasise painting or applied decoration, the visual complexity of Guan ware is often achieved through the interplay of form, glaze thickness, colour, surface texture, and controlled firing conditions.

The present study examines a bamboo-neck vase from a private research collection, designated Sample No. 8.

The vessel is characterised by a rounded lower body, a tall neck articulated by horizontal bamboo-like nodes, a widely flaring mouth, and a semi-matte grey-olive celadon glaze.

The study combines original elemental measurements with a detailed examination of the vessel’s visible surface characteristics and comparative archaeometric research on the technology of the Southern Song official kilns.

The principal aim is to document the chemical composition of the glazed body and to examine its relationship to the material characteristics described for historical Guan ware.

2. Historical Context: The Southern Song Imperial Kilns

2.1. The establishment of official ceramic production

During the Southern Song dynasty (1127–1279) a sophisticated court culture developed in which ceramics played an important ceremonial and aesthetic role.

The establishment of the capital at Lin’an created a need for a reliable supply of high-quality ceramics meeting imperial requirements.

Official kilns were organised to produce vessels for court use, ritual practice, and palace decoration.

The expression Guan yao, literally “official kiln”, refers to this institutionally connected production tradition.

Guan ware production was therefore not simply a local commercial craft. It formed part of a specialised production system serving imperial requirements.

2.2. The Laohudong kiln

The Laohudong (老虎洞, “Tiger Cave”) kiln site lies in the Phoenix Hill area of Hangzhou.

Archaeological investigation has confirmed its importance for understanding Southern Song Guan technology.

Scholars associate the site with the Xiuneisi (修内司), the Palace Maintenance Office, and with historical references to an inner official kiln.

Excavated material provides direct evidence of ceramic bodies, glazes, kiln furniture, and production technology.

Scientific studies of Laohudong have examined glaze composition, glaze thickness, raw materials, firing temperature, and the development of jade-like optical effects.

2.3. The Jiaotanxia kiln

The Jiaotanxia (郊坛下) kiln represents another important centre of Southern Song official ceramic production in Hangzhou.

It is generally regarded as a later or additional official firing facility serving the court.

The surviving archaeological material demonstrates both continuity and variation in Southern Song Guan technology.

Together, Laohudong and Jiaotanxia provide the principal archaeological basis for scientific comparison with the ceramics of the Southern Song official kilns.

2.4. Characteristic technological features

Historical Guan ware is frequently associated with the following features:

  • Thick calcium-bearing celadon glazes.
  • Relatively dark, iron-bearing ceramic bodies.
  • Soft grey-green, blue-green, or grey-olive colouring.
  • Fine or pronounced glaze crackle.
  • Optical effects arising from bubbles and glaze microstructure.
  • Dark exposed foot surfaces.
  • Careful control of vessel proportions and surface appearance.

These characteristics are not identical in all surviving examples. Their significance lies in the technological relationships between them.

3. Description of the Investigated Vase

3.1. Vessel morphology

Sample No. 8 is a ceramic vase with a rounded body and an elongated neck.

The neck is articulated by three projecting horizontal bands resembling the nodes of a bamboo stem.

Above the uppermost node the neck widens into a broad, outward-flaring mouth.

The rounded body visually balances the vertical form of the neck.

The transition between neck and shoulder is emphasised by a raised circular junction.

The bamboo motif is expressed through the architecture of the vessel rather than through painted decoration.

3.2. Glaze colour

The vase is covered with a grey-olive or grey-green celadon glaze.

The surface is smooth to the touch but has a restrained, semi-matte optical lustre.

Its colour changes with lighting and surface curvature, ranging from pale grey-green highlights to darker olive-grey shaded areas.

The glaze is visually continuous over the rounded body and the jointed neck.

3.3. The semi-matte surface

The semi-matte surface is one of the most distinctive characteristics of this object.

Macroscopic photographs show broad, soft highlights rather than uniformly sharp reflections.

The magnified image of the surface shows a fine-grained optical texture.

This visual effect is consistent with light scattering within or on the surface of the glaze.

Possible contributing mechanisms include small bubbles, compositional heterogeneity, microscopic phase separation, and surface roughness.

Photographs alone are not sufficient to determine which of these mechanisms predominates.

3.4. Fine crackle

Fine, locally visible crack-like lines can be seen on the vase.

The pXRF targeting image shows faint linear features in the analysed grey glaze.

The recessed glazed base shows more clearly distinguishable intersecting lines, some of which form irregular polygonal structures.

These observations support the description of a fine crackle network.

The relatively low visual contrast of the cracks distinguishes this vessel from examples with wide, strongly stained crackle.

Some linear marks visible on the base may be surface abrasion rather than glaze cracks. Higher-resolution examination would help to distinguish these features.

3.5. Base morphology

The photograph of the underside shows a broad, light outer ring area, a contrasting dark-brown to almost black concentric zone, and a grey-green central recess.

Fine intersecting lines are visible in some parts of the recessed base.

The dark ring zone recalls the contrasting foot treatments traditionally associated with Guan-type ceramics.

However, the analytical data presented do not include a separate elemental measurement of the dark zone or of the exposed ceramic body.

The dark colour is therefore documented as a morphological and optical characteristic rather than as an independently established iron concentration.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Sample identification

  • Object designation: celadon vase with bamboo neck.
  • Sample ID: 8.
  • Project designation: vaza bambuk.
  • Collection: private research collection.
  • Analysed surface: grey-olive glaze on the vessel body.
  • Analytical method: portable X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy.

4.2. Portable X-ray fluorescence

The vase was examined non-destructively by portable X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy.

The original report identifies the measurement as an in situ analysis.

The instrument reported elemental concentrations and their associated uncertainties, expressed at the level of three standard deviations.

The pXRF targeting image records the area of grey glaze examined by the instrument.

The measurement was made on the body of the vessel, not on the dark ring of the base or on the unglazed ceramic body.

4.3. Optical documentation

The photographic dataset includes:

  • A general view of the bamboo-neck vase.
  • A view of the underside showing the ring structure of the base.
  • A magnified optical image of the grey-green glaze.
  • The targeting image recorded by the pXRF instrument.

These images were used to document vessel morphology, glaze appearance, surface texture, and linear crack-like features.

4.4. Analytical limitations

The pXRF instrument operated in geochemical elemental-analysis mode.

Such measurements are sensitive to the composition of the examined surface but may also be affected by glaze thickness, the ceramic substrate, surface curvature, and matrix effects.

The resulting elemental percentages should not be interpreted as a fully calibrated bulk glaze composition.

Theoretical oxide equivalents are calculated for comparative purposes but are not normalised to 100%.

Raman spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy, and cross-section microanalysis were not carried out within this study.

5.2. Principal chemical observations

The glaze is dominated by silicon and also contains substantial amounts of calcium, aluminium, and potassium.

The calcium concentration of 8.953 wt% is particularly notable.

The iron concentration is considerably lower than the concentrations of the principal glaze-forming elements.

Rubidium and strontium were also detected, providing additional trace-element information for future comparative studies.

The data establish the elemental composition of the measured grey-olive glazed surface.

7. Comparative Archaeometry of Southern Song Guan Glazes

7.1. Technological studies of Laohudong

Wood and Li (2015) investigated the technology used to produce Southern Song Guan ware at Laohudong.

Their study combined analysis of excavated fragments with reconstruction of historical production practices.

The authors emphasised the role of glaze microstructure, especially fine bubbles, in creating the jade-like appearance of Guan ware.

They also discussed multi-layer glazing, the relationship between the glaze and iron-bearing body materials, and the possible use of porcelain stone and calcareous wood ash in glaze preparation.

The study suggested that wood ash may have made up approximately 20–30% of some glaze raw-material mixtures.

The estimated firing conditions corresponded to a final temperature of approximately 1215 °C under the experimental assumptions considered by the authors.

These results are important for interpreting the substantial calcium concentration and subdued optical appearance of Sample No. 8.

Published reference values for Laohudong Guan glaze include approximately:

Notably, the aluminium, calcium, and iron equivalents are close to one another.

The potassium equivalent is somewhat higher than the reference value, while the silicon equivalent is substantially higher.

These differences must be considered in relation to the analytical methods used.

The published archaeological values reflect oxide compositions, whereas the values for Sample No. 8 derive from a surface pXRF measurement.

The table therefore establishes technological comparability rather than quantitative chemical identity.

7.3. Calcium-bearing glaze technology

The high calcium concentration of Sample No. 8 corresponds to the broad spectrum of calcium-bearing silicate glazes used in historical Chinese high-temperature ceramics.

In Guan glaze production, calcium-bearing raw materials played an important role in regulating melting and the physical properties of the glaze.

The substantial calcium signal detected in Sample No. 8 is therefore an important characteristic for comparison with official-kiln glaze technology.

This method does not allow the exact source of the flux to be determined independently.

7.4. Iron and glaze colour

Iron-bearing compounds make an important contribution to the colour of Chinese celadons.

The observed hue depends on several factors, including total iron content, oxidation state, glaze composition, and firing atmosphere.

The grey-olive glaze of Sample No. 8 contains 0.673 wt% elemental iron.

This result confirms the presence of iron in the examined surface.

The specific oxidation states of iron were not determined in this analysis.

Consequently, the exact chromogenic mechanism cannot be established from pXRF measurements alone.

8. Optical Structure and Semi-Matte Appearance

The weak surface reflection of Sample No. 8 is particularly important for the scientific study of Guan glaze.

Wood and Li (2015) demonstrated the importance of glaze microstructure in forming the characteristic optical properties of Laohudong Guan ware.

Later studies examined how glaze composition, iron chemistry, microscopic phases, and crack formation affect the appearance of celadons produced at the Southern Song official kilns.

Ren and colleagues (2026) investigated the mechanisms of colour, opacity, and crack formation in Southern Song Guan glazes.

Their work offers a modern materials-science framework for studying the semi-matte grey-olive surface of Sample No. 8.

The accompanying magnified photograph shows a fine, visually heterogeneous texture.

Such an appearance may result from microscopic light scattering, but the available optical documentation does not allow individual bubbles, crystals, or phase-separated domains to be distinguished.

The value of this observation lies in documenting the relationship between a chemically characterised glaze and its visible optical properties.

9. Fine Crackle and Glaze–Body Stress

The fine crack-like structure of Sample No. 8 is an important feature of its surface morphology.

Crackle in ceramic glazes usually arises from tensile stresses associated with differences in the thermal expansion of glaze and ceramic body.

During cooling, the glaze and the body contract differently.

When the resulting stresses exceed the fracture resistance of the glaze, cracks can form.

In historical Guan ware these cracks became an important element of the finished aesthetic.

Sample No. 8 shows a comparatively fine form of this phenomenon.

Crack-like lines are locally visible in the instrument’s targeting image and more clearly distinguishable in parts of the recessed base.

This network is visually less conspicuous than the large polygonal crackle observed on some other Guan-type vessels.

The difference is important for comparative research, because it demonstrates the diversity of visible crackle morphologies in calcium-bearing celadon systems.

In future, microscopic methods will allow the width, continuity, and intersection geometry of the cracks to be determined, as well as whether the network consists of one or several generations of cracks.

10. Comparative Study of Three Glazed Objects

This research collection includes two further ceramic objects previously examined by X-ray fluorescence:

  • Sample No. 7: a grey crackle-glazed bowl in the form of chrysanthemum petals.
  • Sample No. 1: a grey-green celadon vase with two loop handles and a vertically lobed body, catalogued as Yuan-dynasty Longquan ware.

All three glaze measurements were obtained with the same family of portable X-ray fluorescence instruments and the same analytical mode.

This provides an internally useful comparative dataset.

10.2. Calcium comparison

Among the three examined glazed surfaces, Sample No. 8 shows the highest calcium concentration.

The results are as follows:

  • Bamboo-neck vase No. 8: 8.953 wt% calcium.
  • Yuan Longquan vase No. 1: 7.954 wt% calcium.
  • Chrysanthemum bowl No. 7: 6.954 wt% calcium.

This indicates measurable differences between the glaze surfaces.

Such differences may reflect variations in glaze raw materials, composition, thickness, or analytical interaction with the underlying ceramic material.

10.3. Iron comparison

Iron concentrations also differ:

  • Bamboo-neck vase No. 8: 0.673 wt% Fe.
  • Chrysanthemum bowl No. 7: 0.404 wt% Fe.
  • Yuan Longquan vase No. 1: 1.059 wt% Fe.

The bamboo-neck vase occupies an intermediate position.

This observation is relevant to the study of celadon colour, although the visible hue cannot be predicted from total iron concentration alone.

10.4. Aluminium and potassium

Relatively similar concentrations of aluminium and potassium are observed on all three glazed surfaces.

In aluminium content, Sample No. 8 is particularly close to Sample No. 7.

Potassium values vary within a relatively narrow range across all three measurements.

These relationships provide a useful basis for future multivariate comparative studies within this research programme.

They do not prove that these objects were made in the same kiln or workshop.

11. The Dark Base and the Guan Tradition

The dark exposed foot is one of the frequently discussed features of Southern Song Guan ware.

The historical expression zi kou tie zu (紫口鐵足), traditionally translated as “purple mouth and iron foot”, refers to the visual contrast between thinly glazed or darkened rims and iron-coloured exposed body surfaces.

The underside of Sample No. 8 shows a dark ring zone surrounding the recessed glazed base.

This feature visually corresponds to the Guan tradition.

The surrounding light ring and the central grey-green glaze also indicate a complex distribution of colour and surface treatment at the base.

Because no separate pXRF measurement was made on the dark ring zone, the elemental composition of this area is not determined in this study.

Its technological interpretation therefore still rests on macroscopic appearance and comparative morphology.

12. Scientific Contribution and Originality

The originality of this study lies in the integrated documentation of Sample No. 8.

The study combines:

  • An original pXRF elemental dataset obtained directly from the grey-olive glaze on the vessel body.
  • A detailed morphological description of the bamboo-jointed neck and flaring mouth.
  • Macroscopic observation of the smooth, semi-matte celadon surface.
  • Optical documentation of fine crack-like structures.
  • Photographic examination of the contrasting dark ring base.
  • Comparative analysis with published archaeological studies of Southern Song Guan ware.
  • An internal comparison with two further glazed ceramic objects examined by the same analytical approach.

The study thus makes a new contribution to the study of Chinese celadon technology by providing object-specific chemical and optical data.

Its principal scientific value lies not in a claim that kiln origin can be determined from any single element, but in the documented convergence of glaze chemistry, surface appearance, vessel morphology, and historical technological context.

13. Discussion

The investigated bamboo-neck vase shows a number of features characteristic of Southern Song Guan ceramic technology.

The semi-matte grey-olive glaze is characterised by a substantial calcium content and a relatively low iron concentration.

The fine crackle and subdued surface reflection also correspond to phenomena widely studied in Guan ware.

The bamboo-inspired morphology demonstrates a carefully constructed relationship between natural forms and ceramic design.

The dark ring base is an important comparative feature.

Published archaeometric work provides a technological explanation for several of these observations, in particular the role of calcium-bearing glaze materials, glaze microstructure, and thermal stress.

Comparison with the chrysanthemum-shaped bowl and the Yuan Longquan vase reveals measurable differences in calcium and iron concentrations across the three glazed surfaces.

The bamboo-neck vase has the highest measured calcium concentration and an intermediate iron concentration.

The results establish a useful analytical basis for future research involving repeated measurements, glaze cross-sections, Raman spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and broader archaeological reference datasets.

14. Conclusions

This study presents an original non-destructive analysis of a Southern Song Guan-type celadon vase with a bamboo neck, designated Sample No. 8.

The results of the portable X-ray fluorescence measurement of the grey-olive glaze are:

Si 36.54 wt%, Ca 8.953 wt%, Al 6.52 wt%, K 3.914 wt%, and Fe 0.673 wt%.

The results confirm a calcium-bearing silicate glaze containing measurable iron and potassium.

The vessel is further distinguished by its semi-matte appearance, fine and locally visible crackle, bamboo-jointed neck, flaring mouth, and contrasting dark ring base.

Comparative archaeometric studies of official-kiln production of the Southern Song at Laohudong and Jiaotanxia provide a sound technological context for interpreting these observations.

The calcium and iron characteristics are particularly relevant to historical Guan glaze systems.

Comparison with two further ceramic objects demonstrates measurable differences in glaze chemistry within the research collection.

The principal contribution of this work lies in the original integration of surface elemental analysis, documentation of fine crackle, observation of the semi-matte glaze, and bamboo-inspired vessel morphology within the established scientific framework of Southern Song Guan ceramic technology.

The resulting dataset provides a documented basis for further comparative study of celadon production at the official kilns.
