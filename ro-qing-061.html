Tall Ovoid Celadon Vase with Relief Dragon under Glaze

大清乾隆年製 — Dà Qīng Qiánlóng Nián Zhì · Imperial Monochrome Celadon Porcelain, Late Qianlong Period (1760–1795)

Jade-Toned Celadon Glaze Qing Dynasty · Late Qianlong 1760–1795 Jingdezhen Imperial Kilns Subglaze Five-Clawed Dragon Underglaze-Blue Seal Mark Dronova GOM Method

Object Description

The examined vase belongs to the group of imperial monochrome porcelain of the late Qianlong period (circa 1760–1795), produced at the Imperial kilns of Jingdezhen. The piece is distinguished by an elongated ovoid silhouette, a tall cylindrical neck, and a transparent, jade-toned celadon glaze. Beneath the glaze, a relief of a five-clawed imperial dragon amid cloud scrolls was formed in the porcelain body prior to glazing and high-temperature firing.

The form itself — a tall ovoid body with broad shoulders flowing smoothly into the cylindrical neck, a slightly flared mouth, and a thin-walled, high-density porcelain body — belongs to the group of ceremonial palace vases of the late Qianlong period, characterised by a strong vertical emphasis and harmonious proportions. Beneath the transparent glaze, the relief decoration shows a five-clawed imperial dragon and cloud scrolls formed directly in the porcelain mass, with soft modelling of the relief, no traces of mechanical press-moulding, and a natural softening of the contours under the glaze layer — the relief having been shaped before glazing, which after firing produces the characteristic effect of a decoration "submerged" within the glaze.

The celadon glaze itself is the principal diagnostic feature of the piece. Macroscopically it shows a transparent, glassy structure with a light green tone carrying a slight bluish undertone, a pronounced jade-like optical depth, and high transparency. Colour is naturally intensified within the recesses of the relief and lightens over the raised areas — a distribution linked to variation in glaze thickness during high-temperature firing rather than to any applied pigment.

Fig. 4 — Base. Neatly finished footring of dense white porcelain body with a light cream-sandy tone after firing; the base is evenly covered with glaze, with a six-character underglaze-blue seal mark 大清乾隆年製 (Da Qing Qianlong Nian Zhi) at the centre, executed in correct seal script with a square cartouche of regular proportions.

Technological Interpretation

The interaction between glaze and relief is one of the most informative diagnostic criteria for this vessel: a natural accumulation of glaze within the recesses, a gradual thinning of the glaze over raised areas, a smooth transition between relief and surrounding surface, and no interruption in the continuity of the glaze layer. This differential thickness visually reinforces the volume of the dragon motif and is characteristic of authentic high-temperature celadon glazes from the Imperial kilns of Jingdezhen.

The homogeneous vitreous matrix, high transparency, and absence of secondary coatings observed macroscopically are consistent with a fully fused, high-temperature celadon glaze formed under a controlled reduction atmosphere — the technological standard for imperial celadon production of this period.

The footring shows a neatly finished ring, a dense white porcelain body, a light cream-sandy firing tone, even glaze coverage on the base, and no traces of firing supports or a secondary firing — all consistent with a single, controlled high-temperature firing of a fully finished imperial vessel.

Gemmological Optical Microscopy

No magnified or microscope photographs of the glaze surface were supplied for this object. The observations in this Atlas entry are therefore based entirely on macroscopic examination and standard close-up photography, as documented in Figs. 1–4. This section is included for structural consistency with other entries in the Atlas and should be read as pending rather than complete.

No microscopy imagery available for this entry. A gemmological optical microscopy pass — in particular of the glaze surface over the relief and within its recesses, where the differential glaze thickness described above would be most directly observable at magnification — is recommended before this object is treated as fully documented under the Dronova GOM Method.

Diagnostic Checklist

  • Tall, elongated ovoid body with broad shoulders and a strong vertical emphasis
  • Thin-walled, high-density porcelain body with harmonious ceremonial proportions
  • Subglaze relief of a five-clawed imperial dragon among cloud scrolls, formed before glazing
  • Soft relief modelling with optically softened contours beneath the glaze, no press-moulding traces
  • Transparent, jade-toned celadon glaze with light green colour and slight bluish undertone
  • Natural intensification of glaze colour within relief recesses, lighter tone over raised areas
  • Continuous, unbroken glaze coverage from exterior through the mouth into the interior throat
  • Neatly finished footring, dense white body, light cream-sandy firing tone, fully glazed base
  • Underglaze-blue six-character seal mark Da Qing Qianlong Nian Zhi in correct seal script
  • No microscopy imagery supplied — glaze microstructure not independently verified
  • No elemental (XRF/Raman/SEM-EDS) data yet obtained — the conclusion should be treated as preliminary
By form, manufacturing technology, the character of the subglaze relief, and the qualities of the celadon glaze, the examined vase corresponds to the group of imperial monochrome wares represented in the collections of The Palace Museum (Beijing), the National Palace Museum (Taipei), The Metropolitan Museum of Art, The British Museum, and the Percival David Collection — a group characterised by an elongated ovoid silhouette, a transparent jade-toned celadon glaze, subglaze relief decoration, and underglaze reign marks of the Qianlong period. No microscopy imagery was supplied for this object, so the glaze microstructure could not be independently examined at magnification; this entry should be treated as macroscopically consistent but not yet confirmed at the microscopic or elemental level. A conclusive attribution would require gemmological optical microscopy of the glaze surface, particularly over the relief recesses, together with elemental analysis (XRF/SEM-EDS) of the glaze and body.
